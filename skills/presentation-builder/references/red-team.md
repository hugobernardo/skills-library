# Stage 2.5 — Red team

Read the finished content as the most skeptical person who will see it: someone who wants a reason to say no, has limited time, and knows the domain. Your job is to find the reason before they do.

This runs before design, because design makes weak arguments harder to see and much more expensive to change.

Contents:
1. Find the pass reason
2. Story
3. Argument
4. Evidence classification
5. Copy
6. Slide economy and compression

---

## 1 · Find the pass reason

Start here, because it's the most valuable output of the whole stage. Every presentation has one weakest point where a skeptical audience disengages. Name it explicitly:

> The reason someone says no to this is: ________

Then be honest about whether it's fixable in the deck. Often it isn't — it's a missing piece of evidence, an unsigned agreement, a metric nobody has measured. Saying "this isn't a copy problem, it's a data problem, and here's the one thing that would fix it" is far more useful than rewording a slide.

---

## 2 · Story

- Is the opening compelling, or does it warm up for three slides?
- Where does attention drop? Name the slide.
- What feels predictable — the section a practised audience has seen before?
- Is there a memorable moment where something clicks?
- Does the ending pay off the opening, or just stop?

---

## 3 · Argument

- Which claims are weakest? Rank them by how much damage they do when pushed.
- Which assumptions are hidden — believed by the deck but never stated?
- Where would a knowledgeable skeptic disagree?
- What obvious question does the deck raise and not answer?
- Does any claim get *worse* with time or scrutiny? (A pipeline number that hasn't converted, a partnership that's been "close" for six months.)

Pre-empting an objection you know is coming, in the presenter notes, is worth more than a slide defending it. Volunteering a weakness before it's found buys more credibility than the weakness costs.

---

## 4 · Evidence classification

Grade every load-bearing claim:

| Grade | Meaning | How it should be worded |
|---|---|---|
| **Proven** | Verifiable by a third party today | State it plainly |
| **Reasonably supported** | Real evidence, but thin, small-sample or indirect | State it with its scope and n |
| **Estimate** | Modelled from assumptions | Label it as modelled |
| **Assumption** | Believed, not tested | Say so, or leave it out |
| **Requires evidence** | Needed by the argument, not yet in hand | Flag as a gap with an owner |

Put this in a table in the report. It's usually the part people find most useful, because it converts a vague unease about a deck into a specific list.

Two rules that don't bend. Never allow invented facts, statistics, quotes, sources or case studies. And never let an assumption drift into the deck as a fact — if the grade is assumption, either the wording says so or the claim comes out.

---

## 5 · Copy

Hunt for: jargon, generic language a competitor could also say, overclaiming, unnecessarily long copy, weak headlines, and the machine-written tells listed in `copy-and-visuals.md`.

Check repetition across slides. A phrase that appears three times has become a tic. A framing that appears in two adjacent sections is redundancy.

---

## 6 · Slide economy and compression

Of every slide: **if this disappeared, would the presentation be materially weaker?** If not, recommend removing it. Length is not thoroughness; it's usually an unmade decision.

Then look for compression: the same argument in fewer slides or fewer words without losing meaning. Two slides that share a job should merge. A slide that exists to set up one line should give that line to its neighbour.

Watch specifically for two slides that both define the solution — one in the abstract and one concretely. That's the most common redundancy in a deck and it's easy to miss because both slides seem necessary in isolation.

---

## 7 · Continuity — run this as its own pass

This is the pass that gets skipped, and it catches a class of error nothing else does.

Read the deck straight through as a story, ignoring layout entirely, and ask whether any slide **contradicts** another. Not "is this slide weak" but "does this slide make the previous one false."

Real examples of the kind of thing this catches:
- An opening slide describes a customer as already using the product; a later slide uses that same customer as the example of someone the product hasn't reached yet.
- A slide says a partnership is signed; a later slide lists it as a risk.
- The business model slide implies revenue starts immediately; the plan slide says monetization opens next year.

These survive every other review because each slide is individually fine. Only reading the whole thing as a continuous claim exposes them. A defect pass looks at slides; a continuity pass looks at the argument between them.

---

## 8 · Writing the report

Keep it short and ordered by consequence:

1. **The pass reason** — the one thing most likely to lose the room
2. **Story** — where attention drops, what's predictable, whether the ending pays off
3. **Argument** — weakest claims ranked, hidden assumptions, unanswered questions
4. **Evidence table**
5. **Copy** — specific instances, not general advice
6. **Slide economy** — what to cut, what to merge
7. **Recommended changes**, numbered, so they can be approved individually

Then apply what's approved and stop. Design begins only after the content is signed off.
