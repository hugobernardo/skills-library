# Stage 2 — Copy, visuals and presenter notes

Read this before writing any slide content. Contents:

1. What each slide entry contains
2. Assertion headlines
3. The copy quality standard
4. Choosing a visual mechanism
5. Presenter notes
6. The tells that make copy read as machine-written

---

## 1 · What each slide entry contains

For every slide, produce five things:

**Narrative role** — one sentence reminding both of you what this slide has to accomplish. It comes from the approved architecture; if you can't state it, the slide shouldn't exist.

**Headline** — an assertion, not a label. See below.

**On-slide copy** — the actual words that appear. Usually very little.

**Visual concept** — the mechanism that carries the idea, described precisely enough to build.

**Presenter notes** — what the presenter says, not what the slide says.

---

## 2 · Assertion headlines

A headline should communicate the conclusion, not name the topic.

| Label (weak) | Assertion (strong) |
|---|---|
| Market opportunity | $30B of spending is moving to a channel incumbents weren't built to serve |
| Q3 results | We beat plan on revenue and missed on margin, and the reason is the same |
| Our process | The handoff between design and build is where three of our four delays happen |
| Team | Everyone who has scaled this before is already here |

The reason this matters more than any other copy decision: a deck gets forwarded to people who won't hear the narration. Assertion headlines let the deck argue on its own. They also force clarity on you — if you can't state the conclusion, you don't have one yet, and that's worth discovering at Stage 2 rather than in the room.

Two practical notes. Headlines can run to two lines; a longer sentence that says something beats a short phrase that doesn't. And not every slide can be an assertion — a pure model or definition slide sometimes has to be descriptive. When that happens, notice it and check whether the slide is earning its place, rather than forcing a fake claim onto it.

## 3 · The copy quality standard

Optimize for brevity, specificity, rhythm and memorability. Prefer concrete language to abstraction.

> Instead of: "Unlock omnichannel visibility"
> Prefer: "Put every product on the shelf where online shoppers can actually find it"

Run a dedicated editing pass over every slide and ask:

- Could this be said in fewer words?
- Is every word earning its place?
- Is this specific, or could a competitor say exactly the same thing?
- Does it sound natural read aloud?
- Would an intelligent person actually say this, or is it written-to-be-read-off-a-slide language?
- Is there a clever phrase doing less work than a plain one would?
- Is there jargon hiding an ordinary idea?
- Does the headline carry an actual insight?
- Can the audience get it in three to five seconds?

Cut on sight: generic business jargon, consultant-speak, category clichés, unnecessary adjectives, inflated claims, and any framing you've already used elsewhere in the deck.

Use short copy because the thinking is clear, not because minimalism is fashionable. A slide with four well-chosen words and no idea behind it is worse than a slide with two sentences that land.

## 4 · Choosing a visual mechanism

The visual has to help communicate the idea. Never add an image because a slide "needs a visual" — empty space is better than decoration, and audiences read decoration as padding.

Options worth considering: photograph or full-bleed image · data visualization · direct comparison · timeline · process diagram · flywheel · ecosystem map · product screenshot · before/after · annotated interface · quote · one large number · simple schematic · sequence · physical metaphor · minimal typography.

Favour **one dominant visual idea per slide** over several competing objects. If a slide needs two objects, one should clearly be primary.

When a custom diagram is right, specify: what it represents, its components, their relationships, and what the audience should understand within three seconds. If you can't answer the last one, the diagram is too complicated.

Three habits that raise visual quality quickly:

- **Show the mess.** When the point is that something is messy, complicated or broken, showing the actual artifact beats describing it. A real raw data row next to a clean one argues better than any adjective.
- **Reuse a diagram rather than inventing a second one.** If you build an architecture diagram at slide 8, extend that same diagram at slide 14. The audience already learned to read it, so the addition does the arguing.
- **Let scale be honest.** If a comparison makes your number look small, that's information. Fixing it by distorting the scale is the kind of thing a numerate audience notices instantly.

## 5 · Presenter notes

Notes should carry what the slide deliberately doesn't:

- the core point to make
- the logic connecting this slide to the previous one
- context or evidence that should be spoken rather than printed
- important nuance, caveats, or the honest version of a number
- the transition into the next slide

Never restate what's visible on the slide — that's the most common way notes become useless. Don't write a word-for-word script unless asked; write what lets someone tell the story naturally.

Notes are also where fragile material belongs. A caveat, a sample size, an unsigned partnership, a figure that's modelled rather than measured — spoken, these read as candour and judgment. Printed, they read as fine print and invite a line-by-line audit.

## 6 · The tells that make copy read as machine-written

These are worth actively hunting, because they're the difference between copy that sounds like a person and copy that sounds generated:

- **Manufactured "Not X. Y." constructions.** Occasionally this is genuinely the argument. Usually it's a rhythm trick standing in for a thought.
- **Groups of three used for polish.** Three items because there are three things is fine. Three because it sounds finished is padding — and it's the single most recognizable tell.
- **Em-dash pile-up.** One per paragraph is plenty.
- **Openers like "In today's rapidly evolving landscape."** Delete on sight.
- **Every sentence built as a slogan.** Slogans work by contrast with normal sentences. All-slogan copy reads as a brochure and nothing lands.
- **Abstraction where a concrete noun would do** — "solutions", "capabilities", "leverage", "seamless", "transform".
- **Hedging that softens an actual claim into nothing** — "may potentially help to improve".
- **Repeated framing.** If a good phrase appears on three slides, it stops reading as a callback and starts reading as a tic. Twice is a callback; three times is a habit.

The general test: read it aloud. If you wouldn't say it in a meeting, don't put it on a slide.
