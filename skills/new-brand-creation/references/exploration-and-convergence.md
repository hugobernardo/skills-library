# Exploration and convergence

Use this reference when the brand is not yet selected or when a chosen direction needs pressure-testing.

## 1. Build the real brief

Gather or infer the smallest useful set of inputs:

- What the product or business does in plain language.
- What alternatives get wrong or leave emotionally unsatisfying.
- The desired relationship: tool, expert, confidant, guide, analyst, host, companion, etc.
- The feeling before, during, and after use.
- Behaviors the brand should encourage—and must never perform.
- Existing product concepts or UX that should survive across applications.
- Architecture: standalone brand, umbrella, endorsement, or product family.
- Practical constraints: markets, languages, domains, app stores, legal sensitivity, and launch timing.

Do not mistake a feature list for a brand brief. Translate features into a relationship and a standard of behavior.

## 2. Map territories before names

Create several conceptually distant territories. Each territory should include:

- A central idea or metaphor.
- The emotional register.
- What it would make easy to express.
- Its likely cliché or failure mode.
- A few sample phrases, name shapes, or visual cues—not a finished identity.

Use at least one territory meaningfully distant from the user's starting point. Distance should still connect to product truth; randomness is not originality.

Useful sources of distance include publishing, geometry, domestic rituals, temporal language, music, materials, cartography, botany, tools, and ordinary speech. These are prompts, not mandatory categories.

## 3. Read feedback as constraints

Track four signals:

- **Pull:** what attracts the user and why.
- **Push:** what feels false, preachy, generic, heavy, self-deprecating, literal, or over-ambitious.
- **Neutral:** technically acceptable but not distinctive.
- **Contradiction:** a proposed idea conflicts with product behavior or with another stated preference.

Reason matters more than the vote. “I like it” may reveal tone, meaning, rhythm, visual potential, or familiarity; ask only when the distinction would change the next round.

Do not average contradictory preferences into beige. Name the tension and seek a direction that resolves it.

## 4. Generate and screen names

Generate within the strongest territories, then present compact groups with short rationales. Vary linguistic construction as well as meaning: ordinary phrases, editorial terms, spatial relationships, coined words, altered compounds, and precise technical language can create very different identities.

Screen promising names for:

- Fit with the product relationship and behavioral promise.
- Sound, spelling, recall, and spoken ambiguity.
- Semantic depth without requiring an essay.
- Tone in context, including accidental threat, judgment, grandiosity, or self-deprecation.
- Stretch across likely product evolution.
- Exact and near-match commercial use in relevant categories.
- App-store, domain, social, and search-result confusion.
- Obvious linguistic issues in priority markets.

An available domain is evidence, not a verdict. A parked `.com` does not prove a name is clear; an unavailable exact `.com` does not automatically kill an excellent non-company product name.

Recommend one name when the evidence supports it. Preserve alternatives only when a real unresolved tradeoff remains.

## 5. Lock the verbal core

Before visual convergence, state:

- **Name**
- **Descriptor:** what it is, without marketing fog.
- **Brand line:** the central promise or point of view.
- **Character:** a few behavioral traits expressed as actions, not adjectives alone.
- **Voice rules:** how it explains, asks, reassures, warns, and admits uncertainty.
- **Boundary statement:** what the brand may do and what it must not turn into.

The boundary statement prevents later brand theater. Examples of the pattern:

> The product may reveal a pattern. It does not turn that pattern into an instruction without being asked.

> The product may answer the question. It does not turn the answer into a judgment.

Write a boundary specific to the product rather than copying these formulations.

## 6. Explore visual identity

Build a small number of visually distinct studies. Show the mark in context: wordmark, app icon or favicon, light/dark reproduction, small-size test, and one plausible product or business surface.

Evaluate:

- Distinctiveness versus category defaults and generic SaaS geometry.
- Connection to the brand idea without literal illustration.
- Unintended readings and cultural associations.
- Small-size legibility and silhouette.
- Reproduction in one color and on light/dark surfaces.
- Whether the mark still works without an explanatory story.
- Whether the wordmark carries the appropriate weight and tone.

Prefer one meaningful final refinement over endless cosmetic iteration. If the latest direction is weaker, return to the prior approved version rather than defending sunk work.

## 7. Build the system around product behavior

Define semantic roles, not just swatches:

- Primary and supporting colors, backgrounds, text, borders, data and accent roles.
- Typography roles for conclusions, facts, long-form text, controls, and numbers.
- Spacing, radius, divider, elevation, and motion principles when relevant.
- Imagery, illustration, iconography, or editorial ornament rules.
- Voice examples for normal, empty, uncertain, error, and high-stakes states.
- Accessibility and evidence/provenance rules.

Avoid assigning moral meaning to decorative colors unless the product genuinely requires a validated semantic status system. Calm design must not hide uncertainty, risk, or missing data.

## 8. Stage gates

Do not move forward merely because material exists. Converge when:

- The concept fits the product's behavior, not only its aesthetics.
- The user can articulate why the direction feels right.
- Major rejection reasons have been resolved rather than renamed.
- The name has passed a practical conflict screen.
- The mark has survived context and size tests.
- The identity can guide implementation without dictating every product decision.
